# Tangible Fieldwork

Tangible Fieldwork treats ideas as things that can be handled, compared, opened, and put to work. Form is grounded in familiar tools, modest architecture, paper systems, and small physical models rather than abstract technological spectacle. Space is generous and deliberate: each object earns its position, and the calm around it is as important as the object itself.

Colour comes from useful materials—oat paper, warm timber, mineral plaster, forest cloth, oxidised clay, muted sky, and sparing brass. Emerald is structural rather than decorative, used to bind the family together and guide attention. Every tonal relationship should feel painstakingly calibrated, as though the palette were tested under real daylight over many hours.

Scale moves between the workbench and the world it describes. A building can become a desk model; a workflow can become a sequence of paper tokens; a practical service can be understood through the objects it uses. Rhythm comes from repeated folios, cards, tools, and specimen-like groupings, with each variation meticulously crafted so repetition builds meaning instead of monotony.

Composition should feel editorial and observant. Objects overlap with believable depth, quiet shadows, and tactile edges, while the frame preserves enough breathing room for the eye to understand the whole before studying the details. Text is sparse and clinical when present: a title, an index, a short label—never a paragraph competing with the visual story.

The final work should look laboured over by a master craftsperson: no arbitrary embellishment, no generic icon collage, and no unresolved corner. Precision lives in crop, alignment, paper grain, object scale, and the balance between order and warmth. The result is an art object first—a coherent field guide whose visual language carries the information before words are needed.
